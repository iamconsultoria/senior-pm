# Tailoring — §3, pp. 103–112

Deliberate adaptation of approach, governance and processes to fit the project's environment
and objectives. Covers development approach, processes, life cycle, deliverables, and the
approach to stakeholder and team involvement.

## What to tailor — §3.3, p. 104
Three targets: **life cycle and development approach selection** (104), **processes** (105),
**engagement** (105).

Engagement is the forgotten one. Lightening the process without changing who is involved in it
produces a lighter process running at the old speed, and everyone concludes tailoring doesn't
work.

## The process — §3.4, p. 105
Four steps. Detail Figure 3-1, p. 106 · org and project factors Figure 3-2, p. 107 · overall
flow Figure 3-3, p. 110 · fitting the context Figure 3-4, p. 111.

1. **Select initial development approach** (105) → `03-lifecycle.md`
2. **Tailor for the organization** (106) — governance, standards, audit exposure, existing
   methodology
3. **Tailor for the project** (108) — size, complexity, criticality, team, stakeholders
4. **Implement ongoing improvement** (109) — not a one-time setup

The summary (112) is explicit on two things worth quoting back to people: tailoring is
**typically led by project stakeholders**, with organisational guidelines and governance
keeping it consistent; and it requires **verifying and validating that the tailored approach
actually worked**. Tailoring without a feedback loop is just deviation with better vocabulary.

## Diagnostics — §3.6, p. 110

**Table 3-1, p. 112 — Common Situations and Tailoring Suggestions.** The DIAGNOSE engine,
restated:

| Symptom | Intervention |
|---|---|
| Deliverables are poor quality | Analyse root causes; apply targeted feedback and quality assurance where needed. Improve the **process** rather than adding verification steps. |
| Team unsure how to proceed | Assess what the team actually needs — guidance, mentorship, training, knowledge-sharing — and supply that specific thing. |
| Team uncooperative, working in silos | Team-building plus individual conversations to find the cause; then act to strengthen bonds and coordination. |
| Long delays waiting for approvals | Appoint people authorised to decide up to defined value thresholds. Shorten the chain rather than escalating faster. |
| Too much work in progress, or waste | Visualise the work — value stream mapping, kanban — to expose where it accumulates, then solve what you can see. |
| Stakeholders disengaged or negative | Check whether they receive timely, relevant information; re-tailor communication methods, balancing simplicity against the need for depth. |
| No transparency on progress | Verify the right data is collected, analysed, shared and discussed in team and stakeholder meetings; agree the measures with team and stakeholders. |
| Recurring surprise issues and risks forcing reaction | Explore root causes to find the gaps in project processes or activities producing them. |

**Note the shape of every one.** Find the cause, change the system. Not one says "add a
control" or "report more often." When you diagnose, resist prescribing more process — the
standard consistently does not, and the instinct to add governance in response to trouble is
what turns a struggling project into a slow struggling project.

## The over-processing test
Before recommending any artifact or ceremony, ask: what decision does this change? If nothing,
it is overhead. The guide's own framing is that the 40 processes are illustrative, not
prescriptive. A two-week internal initiative needs a goal, an owner, a check-in and a
definition of done. Say that plainly rather than scaling the framework down politely.
